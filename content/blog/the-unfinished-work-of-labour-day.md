There is a peculiar silence at the heart of the first Monday in September. We call it Labour Day, and yet for most of us it arrives as little more than the soft closing of summer — one last long weekend before the year's machinery resumes in earnest. The barbecues are lit, the cottages are emptied, and the word *labour* passes across our lips without ever touching the thing it names. This year, reading Adam D.K. King's essay in *The Maple* (King, 2026), I found myself arrested by a simple inversion: Labour Day should not only be a day *off* work. It should be a day *about* work — about who performs it, who profits from it, and how little power working people actually wield over the conditions of their own lives.

It is worth pausing on how thoroughly the modern world rests on hands we rarely see. Workers build the homes we sleep in, lay the infrastructure beneath our feet, manufacture and move our goods, tend our sick and our elderly, teach our children, grow and prepare our food, and carry out the endless paid and unpaid tasks by which society reproduces itself, day after ordinary day (King, 2026). The old labour anthem "Solidarity Forever" put it with a bluntness that no economics textbook has ever improved upon: "Without our brain and muscle not a single wheel can turn" (Chaplin, 1915). And yet the people whose labour turns those wheels exercise remarkably little control over the economy they sustain. That asymmetry — between what workers give and what workers govern — is what the holiday was always meant to confront.

## A Holiday Born on a Picket Line

The history of Labour Day in Canada makes this plainer than any theory could. In the spring of 1872, members of the Toronto Typographical Union struck for the nine-hour workday, at a time when working people commonly laboured ten or more hours daily and when union activity itself could still be prosecuted as criminal conspiracy (King, 2026). When Toronto's publishers refused the printers' demands, the workers walked off the job, and some 10,000 workers and supporters marched to Queen's Park in solidarity. Employers answered in the familiar manner of every era since: they hired scabs and had strike leaders arrested. But the repression backfired. Within days, Prime Minister John A. Macdonald introduced the legislation that became the Trade Union Act, 1872, providing the first legal protection for union organization in Canada (Trade Union Act, 1872). The strike also helped propel the broader Nine Hour Movement, fixing shorter working time at the very centre of the emerging Canadian labour movement's demands.

What followed is instructive. Annual workers' parades spread across the country through the 1880s, occasions on which tradespeople could display the dignity of their crafts and demonstrate, in public and in numbers, the growing social weight of organized labour. By 1894, under sustained pressure from the movement, the federal government formally recognized Labour Day as a national holiday (Heron & Penfold, 2005). A holiday, in other words, was not handed down from above as an act of benevolence. It was *won* — extracted, in the end, by people willing to risk arrest for the audacious proposition that their lives belonged, at least partly, to themselves.

## The Respectable Festival

Honesty requires acknowledging the other side of this history. By the late nineteenth century, May Day had emerged internationally as the more militant working-class holiday, bound up with the campaign for the eight-hour day and increasingly with socialist and revolutionary politics. An officially sanctioned September holiday offered governments — and the more conservative currents within labour itself — a respectable alternative (King, 2026). Labour historians Craig Heron and Steve Penfold (2005) have shown, in their definitive study of the holiday, how Labour Day developed as an uneasy compound of working-class solidarity, public respectability, and organized leisure. Institutionalizing the September festival could serve, at least in part, to domesticate working-class politics — to replace the confrontational memory of May Day with a celebration the state could safely endorse.

Yet to dismiss the September holiday as merely an establishment invention would be to erase its deeper origins in genuine working-class struggle. The printers of 1872 were not respectability's servants; they were its inconvenience. The better response to an ambivalent history is not abandonment but reclamation — to insist that the day recover the political meaning it was born carrying (King, 2026).

## The Present Is Not Finished with the Past

If Labour Day is to mean anything, it must speak to the fights in front of us, and there is no shortage of them. Most immediately, the right to strike — the very right whose suppression sparked the holiday's origin story — is again under pressure. The federal government's increasingly interventionist posture toward major labour disputes has generated serious alarm among unions; repeated intervention risks transforming the right to strike into a right workers hold only until exercising it becomes economically or politically inconvenient (King, 2026).

Meanwhile, employers have discovered ways to sidestep conventional employment protections altogether. Statistics Canada (2024) estimates that 927,000 Canadians performed some form of work through a digital platform in 2023, and that 468,000 were paid directly through a platform or app. Gig work raises a foundational question — who, exactly, counts as an employee? — and with it, who is entitled to minimum employment standards, workers' compensation, Employment Insurance, or any meaningful access to collective bargaining. Migrant workers face a parallel architecture of vulnerability: employer-specific work permits tie a worker's authorization to remain and work in Canada to a single employer, systemically exposing them to abuse, and the reach of such permits continues to grow across sectors (King, 2026). The holiday's demand, it turns out, has not aged; the economy has simply found new shapes for the old evasion.

## An Economy That Keeps Labour Cheap

Defending existing rights, however necessary, is not the same as advancing. Labour Day should also be the occasion on which the labour movement articulates demands capable of improving life for the entire working class, not only its organized fraction. Wages are the natural starting point. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) defines low-paid work as earnings below two-thirds of the median wage, and its comparative data have consistently placed Canada among the countries with substantial low-wage employment — 19.5 per cent of full-time workers classified as low paid (King, 2026; OECD, n.d.). An economy in which nearly one in five full-time workers cannot reliably attain a decent living is not an economy that has failed to reward work; it is an economy organized around keeping labour cheap.

Unionization is the other obvious frontier. Statistics Canada data reported this year put collective agreement coverage at 30.6 per cent of Canadian employees in 2025, with a chasm between sectors: 77.5 per cent of public-sector workers were covered, compared with just 15.1 per cent in the private sector (King, 2026). Compare the Nordic countries, where OECD figures place union density at roughly 60 per cent in Denmark and 66 per cent in Sweden, with collective agreements covering some 88 per cent of Swedish workers (OECD, n.d.). Some Canadian jurisdictions have restored card-check certification to ease access to bargaining, and that matters. But if private-sector density is ever to be repaired, Canada will need sectoral bargaining — the ability of workers to negotiate standards across entire industries rather than workplace by beleaguered workplace (King, 2026).

## The Oldest Demand, Made New

There is something fitting, even poetic, about returning to the demand that gave Canadian Labour Day its origin: time itself. More than 150 years ago, Toronto printers struck because they believed workers deserved more of their lives outside of work. There is no reason that demand should be interred as history. Canada remains strikingly stingy with statutory vacation: in most provinces, workers receive two weeks after a year of continuous employment, rising to three only after further years of service. European Union rules, by contrast, guarantee every worker at least four weeks of paid annual leave (Directive 2003/88/EC, 2003), and Sweden mandates twenty-five days annually, with many industry-wide agreements providing more (Semesterlagen, 1977). The same comparative shortfalls appear in sick leave, parental and caregiving leave, and nearly every other form of paid time away from work (King, 2026). There is no good reason — no reason beyond the inertia of an economy built on other people's hours — that Canadian workers should accept surrendering so much more of their one life to the workplace.

So let the demand be stated plainly: shorter workweeks and workdays without loss of pay, substantially more paid vacation, stronger paid sick leave, and expanded family and caregiving leave (King, 2026). The four-day week, in particular, could graduate from isolated experiment to central labour movement demand. These are also precisely the kinds of demands around which a renewed Labour Day politics could cohere, because they lift everyone. Labour movements have always been strongest when they fought beyond the boundaries of their own bargaining units — universal health care, public pensions, minimum employment standards, workers' compensation, and the minimum wage were all won this way, raising the floor beneath the entire working class (King, 2026). Shorter working time carries the same universal promise. So do higher minimum wages, easier access to unionization, and employment protections that follow the worker regardless of whether an employer chooses to label them an employee, a contractor, or a "partner" on an app.

## Reclaiming the Day

The printers of 1872 understood something we keep having to relearn: that workers should not have to trade away nearly all of their waking lives in exchange for a wage, and that technological and economic progress should mean something more than greater output and higher profits for those at the top (King, 2026). More than a century and a half later, that principle remains unfinished business.

This Labour Day, then, I find myself resisting the holiday's quiet drift toward pure leisure — not because rest is unworthy, but because rest was the *demand*, not the meaning. The meaning was, and remains, collective: that the people who turn the wheels should have a say in where the machine is going. Higher wages, broader collective bargaining, full rights for migrant and gig workers, a stronger right to strike, more paid time off — these are class-wide demands, and every worker stands to gain from them (King, 2026). On the first Monday in September, we should not merely celebrate past victories or stand guard over them. We should organize to win the next ones.
