1. Arithmetic, signs, fractions, and powersMaths toolkit

Maths toolkit
Maths toolkitOverview
Maths toolkit1.1 Order of operations

Use brackets → powers/roots → multiplication/division → addition/subtraction. Multiplication and division have equal priority, as do addition and subtraction; work left to right within the same priority.

2 + 3 × 4 = 14, but (2 + 3) × 4 = 20.

Circuit consequence: 1/R_eq = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ means add the two reciprocals first, then take the reciprocal of the sum. It does not mean R_eq = R₁ + R₂.

Maths toolkit1.2 Directed numbers
  • a − (−b) = a + b: subtracting a negative adds.
  • (−a)(−b) = +ab; (−a)(+b) = −ab.
  • A negative voltage or current is usually information about direction, not a failed calculation.

Example: if you assume 2 A clockwise and obtain i = −0.3 A, the physical current is 0.3 A counterclockwise.

Maths toolkit1.3 Fractions and ratios
  • a/b + c/d = (ad + bc)/(bd)
  • (a/b) ÷ (c/d) = ad/bc
  • 1/(a/b) = b/a
  • A ratio a:b = 2:3 means a/b = 2/3; the total is 5 parts.

Circuit use: conductance is G = 1/R, measured in siemens (S). Two parallel resistors are easiest in conductance form: G_eq = G₁ + G₂.

Mini-example: for 2 kΩ and 4 kΩ in parallel, 1/R_eq = 1/2000 + 1/4000 = 3/4000 S, so R_eq = 4000/3 Ω = 1.333 kΩ.

Maths toolkit2.3 Scientific notation and conversion

A number in scientific notation is a × 10ⁿ, usually with 1 ≤ |a| < 10. Moving the decimal point changes the exponent in the opposite direction.

  • 0.00047 = 4.7 × 10⁻⁴
  • 3.2 × 10⁵ = 320,000
  • 470 μA = 470 × 10⁻⁶ A = 4.7 × 10⁻⁴ A

Reliable exam routine: convert every given value to base SI units before substitution; compute; then convert the answer to a convenient prefix.

Maths toolkit2.4 Dimensional checks

Both sides of an equation must have the same units. Useful derived units:

  • 1 V = 1 J/C
  • 1 A = 1 C/s
  • 1 W = 1 J/s = 1 V·A
  • 1 Ω = 1 V/A
  • 1 F = 1 C/V
  • 1 H = 1 V·s/A
  • R C has units seconds; L/R also has units seconds.

Example: in τ = R_eq C, (V/A)(C/V) = C/A = s, so the formula is dimensionally plausible. A dimensional match does not prove the algebra is correct, but a mismatch proves an error.

3. Algebra you must be able to do by handMaths toolkit

Maths toolkit
Maths toolkitOverview
Maths toolkit3.1 Rearranging an equation

An equation stays true if you perform the same valid operation on both sides. The goal is to isolate the unknown.

Ohm’s law triangle, without memorizing three separate facts:

V = IR reads “voltage equals current times resistance.”

  • Divide by R: I = V/R.
  • Divide by I: R = V/I.

Example: find R when V = 9 V and I = 30 mA. Convert 30 mA = 0.030 A; then R = 9/0.030 = 300 Ω.

Maths toolkit3.2 Expanding, factoring, and clearing fractions
  • Distributive law: a(b + c) = ab + ac.
  • Difference of squares: a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b).
  • To clear fractions, multiply every term by the least common denominator.
  • Keep brackets around substituted negative values and multi-term expressions.

Nodal example: (v − 12)/2000 + v/4000 = 0. Multiply by 4000: 2(v − 12) + v = 0; hence 3v = 24, so v = 8 V.

Maths toolkit3.3 Quadratics and the discriminant

For as² + bs + c = 0, with a ≠ 0:

s = [−b ± √(b² − 4ac)]/(2a)

Read: “s equals minus b, plus or minus the square root of b squared minus four a c, all over two a.”

The discriminant is Δ = b² − 4ac:

  • Δ > 0: two distinct real roots.
  • Δ = 0: one repeated real root.
  • Δ < 0: two complex-conjugate roots.

This directly determines overdamped, critically damped, and underdamped RLC responses.

Maths toolkit3.4 Simultaneous linear equations

A system has several equations that must all be true at once. In circuits, unknowns are usually node voltages or mesh currents.

Two-variable elimination recipe:

  1. Write both equations in a₁x + b₁y = c₁ form.
  2. Multiply one or both equations so one variable has equal-magnitude coefficients.
  3. Add or subtract to eliminate that variable.
  4. Solve the remaining one-variable equation.
  5. Back-substitute to find the other variable.
  6. Check both original equations.

Worked example:

2v₁ − v₂ = 6
−v₁ + 3v₂ = 2

Multiply the second by 2: −2v₁ + 6v₂ = 4. Add to the first: 5v₂ = 10, so v₂ = 2. Then 2v₁ − 2 = 6, so v₁ = 4. Check: −4 + 3(2) = 2. If these are node voltages, the answers are v₁ = 4 V, v₂ = 2 V.

Exam discipline: first obtain one correct equation per independent unknown; only then do arithmetic. Do not mix equation setup and mental rearrangement.

Maths toolkit3.5 Proportionality and sanity checks
  • If y = kx, doubling x doubles y.
  • If y = k/x, doubling x halves y.
  • If y = kx², doubling x multiplies y by 4.

Use these to check formulas: at fixed R, resistor power P = V²/R quadruples when V doubles; at fixed V, it halves when R doubles.

4. Graphs, slopes, areas, and piecewise waveformsMaths toolkit

Maths toolkit
Maths toolkitOverview
Maths toolkit4.1 Straight lines

y = mx + c reads “y equals m x plus c.” m is slope and c is the y-intercept.

m = Δy/Δx = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁)

Read Δ as “change in”: final minus initial. Slope measures how quickly y changes as x changes.

  • Positive slope: rising line.
  • Negative slope: falling line.
  • Zero slope: horizontal line; the quantity is constant.
  • A vertical ideal jump has undefined finite slope and requires special circuit interpretation.
Maths toolkit4.2 Why circuits use slopes and areas
  • Capacitor current is proportional to the slope of capacitor voltage.
  • Inductor voltage is proportional to the slope of inductor current.
  • Capacitor voltage change is proportional to the area under capacitor current.
  • Inductor current change is proportional to the area under inductor voltage.

Thus a constant value has zero slope, but it need not be zero itself.

Maths toolkit4.3 Areas by hand
  • Rectangle: A = width × height.
  • Triangle: A = ½ × base × height.
  • Trapezoid: A = ½(sum of parallel sides) × width.

Areas below the time axis are negative. Add signed areas over each interval, then include the initial value.

Piecewise method: split at every corner or switching time; handle one simple segment at a time; carry the final value of one segment forward as the initial value of the next.

Maths toolkit4.4 Reading `0⁻`, `0⁺`, and `∞`
  • x(0⁻): value immediately before the event at t = 0.
  • x(0⁺): value immediately after the event.
  • x(∞): final steady value long after the event, when transients have decayed.

These are limits, not three separate clocks. For capacitor voltage and inductor current with finite excitation, x(0⁺) = x(0⁻).

Maths toolkit5.1 The exponential function

eˣ is the exponential function, with e ≈ 2.71828. Its defining calculus property is that its derivative is itself: d(eˣ)/dx = eˣ.

For τ > 0, e⁻ᵗᐟτ starts at 1 when t = 0 and decays toward 0. A larger τ means slower decay.

Maths toolkit5.2 Natural logarithms

ln is the inverse of e: if y = eˣ, then ln y = x.

  • ln(ab) = ln a + ln b
  • ln(a/b) = ln a − ln b
  • ln(aⁿ) = n ln a
  • ln e = 1, ln 1 = 0

Find a time from a decay: if 0.10 = e⁻ᵗᐟτ, then ln 0.10 = −t/τ, so t = −τ ln 0.10 ≈ 2.303τ.

Maths toolkit5.3 Minimum trigonometry for underdamped circuits

Angles in derivatives and RLC formulas are in radians, not degrees. 2π radians = one full cycle.

  • sin 0 = 0, cos 0 = 1.
  • d(sin ωt)/dt = ω cos ωt.
  • d(cos ωt)/dt = −ω sin ωt.
  • Period T_d = 2π/ω_d.

For x(t) = e⁻ᵅᵗ[A cos(ω_d t) + B sin(ω_d t)], the sine/cosine creates oscillation and e⁻ᵅᵗ shrinks its envelope.

Maths toolkit5.4 Minimum complex numbers

In electrical engineering, j = √−1, so j² = −1. A complex number is z = a + jb, where a is the real part and b is the imaginary part.

  • Add/subtract real and imaginary parts separately.
  • (a + jb)(a − jb) = a² + b².
  • The conjugate of a + jb is a − jb.
  • Magnitude: |z| = √(a² + b²).
  • Euler’s identity: eʲθ = cos θ + j sin θ.

Only midterm use here: interpreting negative-discriminant roots and converting them to real damped sinusoids. You do not need phasor arithmetic for this scope.

Maths toolkit6.3 What an ODE says

An ordinary differential equation (ODE) relates a quantity and its derivatives with respect to one variable, here time.

  • First order: highest derivative is dx/dt; one independent storage state normally gives one initial condition.
  • Second order: highest derivative is d²x/dt²; two independent storage states normally require x(0⁺) and x′(0⁺).
  • Linear, constant coefficient: x and its derivatives appear to power 1, without products such as x dx/dt, and coefficients are constant.
  • Homogeneous/source-free: forcing term is zero.
  • Forced: an independent source produces a nonzero forcing term.

The complete response is the part due to stored initial energy plus the part due to sources. For stable DC circuits it can also be viewed as a decaying transient plus a constant final steady state.

Maths toolkit6.4 Solving linear constant-coefficient ODEs
  1. Write the ODE in standard form.
  2. Find the constant particular/final response for DC forcing.
  3. Set the forcing term to zero and substitute x_tr = Ke^{st}.
  4. Divide by Ke^{st} to obtain the characteristic equation in s.
  5. Solve for roots; select the response form.
  6. Add transient and final response.
  7. Apply initial conditions to the complete response, not the transient alone.
  8. Verify the ODE, initial conditions, final value, and units.

7. Charge, current, voltage, energy, and powerPhysics & Circuits 0

Physics & Circuits 0
Physics & Circuits 0Overview
Physics & Circuits 07.1 Charge and current

Charge q is a physical property measured in coulombs. Conventional current is the rate at which positive charge passes a point:

i = dq/dt

Read: “current equals the rate of change of charge with time.” For constant current, I = Q/t, so Q = It.

  • Current flows through an element or wire.
  • Conventional current points opposite to electron drift in a metal.
  • A nonzero steady current needs a closed conducting path.
  • Series elements carry the same current because there is only one path.

Example: Q = 0.5 C passes in t = 2 s, so average current I = Q/t = 0.25 A.

Physics & Circuits 07.2 Voltage = energy per unit charge
v = dw/dq

Read: “voltage equals energy transferred per unit charge.” For a constant voltage, W = QV.

  • Voltage is measured across two points: v_AB = v_A − v_B, “potential at A minus potential at B.”
  • A node voltage is measured relative to a chosen reference node, usually called ground and assigned 0 V.
  • Ground is a reference choice; it does not necessarily mean a physical connection to Earth.
  • Parallel elements have the same voltage because they connect the same two nodes.

Bounded water analogy: voltage is like a pressure difference and current like flow rate. This helps intuition only; electric charge and fields are not literally water.

Physics & Circuits 07.3 Energy and power
p = dw/dt

Read: “power is the rate of energy transfer.” Therefore w = ∫p dt; for constant power, W = Pt.

Under the passive sign convention, current enters the element’s positive voltage terminal and:

p = vi
  • p > 0: element absorbs power (dissipates or stores energy).
  • p < 0: element supplies power.
  • If current enters the negative terminal instead, use p = −vi.

For a resistor:

p = vi = i²R = v²/R

The last two forms already assume positive resistance and do not require a separate source-supply sign rule.

Power audit: with consistent passive signs, the algebraic sum of all element powers is zero: Σp = 0. Equivalently, total power supplied equals total power absorbed.

Physics & Circuits 07.4 Passive-sign worked example

A 100 Ω resistor has v = 10 V, with current entering its + terminal.

i = v/R = 10/100 = 0.10 A
p = vi = 10(0.10) = 1.0 W

Check: i²R = (0.10)²(100) = 1.0 W. The resistor absorbs 1 W. In 5 s at constant power it converts W = Pt = 5 J to heat.

9. Ohm’s law, resistance, KCL, and KVLPhysics & Circuits 0

Physics & Circuits 0
Physics & Circuits 0Overview
Physics & Circuits 09.1 Ohm’s law

With current entering the + terminal:

v = iR

Read: “the resistor voltage drop in the current direction equals current times resistance.”

If your defined current enters the − terminal, then v = −iR for that labeled polarity. Many errors come not from Ohm’s law but from inconsistent arrows and polarity marks.

For a uniform physical resistor:

R = ρℓ/A

ρ (rho) is resistivity in Ω·m, ℓ is length, and A is cross-sectional area. Longer increases R; thicker decreases R. This physical formula is secondary to circuit analysis but explains resistance.

Physics & Circuits 09.2 Kirchhoff’s current law (KCL)

KCL is conservation of charge at a node or closed boundary:

Σi_entering = Σi_leaving, or Σi = 0 with one consistent sign choice.

Recipe: choose “leaving positive” (or entering positive); give every connected branch its correct sign; substitute element laws only after the KCL structure is correct.

Example: 3 A and i enter a node, while 1 A and 4 A leave. Then 3 + i = 1 + 4, so i = 2 A.

Physics & Circuits 09.3 Kirchhoff’s voltage law (KVL)

KVL is conservation of energy around a closed loop:

Σv_rises = Σv_drops, or Σv = 0.

Recipe: choose a direction around the loop; crossing from − to + is a rise; + to − is a drop; return to the start; keep all signs.

Example: a 12 V source drives one series loop with resistor drops v₁ and v₂. KVL gives 12 − v₁ − v₂ = 0, so the resistor drops sum to 12 V.

Physics & Circuits 09.4 Combined one-loop example

A 12 V ideal source drives R₁ = 20 Ω and R₂ = 40 Ω in series.

  1. Series resistance: R_eq = 60 Ω.
  2. Ohm’s law: i = 12/60 = 0.20 A.
  3. Drops: v₁ = iR₁ = 4 V; v₂ = iR₂ = 8 V.
  4. KVL check: 12 − 4 − 8 = 0.
  5. Power supplied by source: 12(0.20) = 2.4 W.
  6. Resistor power: i²R₁ + i²R₂ = 0.8 + 1.6 = 2.4 W.

10. Series, parallel, and network reductionPhysics & Circuits 0

Physics & Circuits 0
Physics & Circuits 0Overview
Physics & Circuits 010.1 Recognition before formulas
  • Series: elements share exactly one node and that shared node connects to nothing else; same current flows through each.
  • Parallel: every element connects directly between the same two nodes; same voltage appears across each.
  • Elements drawn side by side are not automatically parallel; elements drawn in a line are not automatically series if a branch joins the middle node.

Reduction recipe: mark the two terminals; label all nodes; redraw; reduce only genuine series/parallel groups; keep track of where the requested variable went; repeat until one equivalent remains.

Do not use a simplified equivalent to recover an internal voltage/current that disappeared during reduction. Return to earlier stages or use the divider rules.

Physics & Circuits 010.3 Voltage division

For series resistors with total voltage v_s across the whole series chain:

v_k = v_s R_k/ΣR

Read: “the voltage across resistor k equals total voltage times its fraction of total resistance.” Larger series resistance gets a larger voltage drop.

Condition: the resistors must be in series. A load connected at the output changes the network and must be included.

Physics & Circuits 010.4 Current division

For two parallel resistors carrying total current i_s into the pair:

i₁ = i_s R₂/(R₁ + R₂)
i₂ = i_s R₁/(R₁ + R₂)

The current through one branch uses the other branch’s resistance. Smaller resistance takes more current.

General parallel form using conductances:

i_k = i_s G_k/ΣG
Physics & Circuits 010.5 Worked reduction and division

A 12 V source feeds R₁ = 2 kΩ in series with the parallel pair R₂ = 4 kΩ, R₃ = 4 kΩ.

  1. R₂ ∥ R₃ = (4 × 4)/(4 + 4) kΩ = 2 kΩ.
  2. Total: R_eq = 2 + 2 = 4 kΩ.
  3. Source current: i_s = 12/4000 = 3 mA.
  4. Parallel-pair voltage: 12 × 2/(2 + 2) = 6 V.
  5. Each 4 kΩ branch current: 3 mA × 4/(4 + 4) = 1.5 mA.
  6. Checks: branch currents sum to 3 mA; drops are 6 V + 6 V = 12 V.

11. Nodal analysisCircuits 1 methods

Circuits 1 methods
Circuits 1 methodsOverview
Circuits 1 methods11.1 Core idea

Nodal analysis uses node voltages as unknowns and KCL to write equations. A branch current from node a to node b through resistor R is:

i_{a→b} = (v_a − v_b)/R

Read: “current from a to b equals the starting potential minus the ending potential, divided by resistance.”

Circuits 1 methods11.2 Standard recipe
  1. Choose a reference node (0 V), preferably one joined to many branches or a voltage source.
  2. Label unknown node voltages relative to reference.
  3. If a voltage source joins a node to ground, that node voltage is known directly.
  4. At each independent unknown node, write KCL.
  5. Replace resistor currents with voltage differences divided by R.
  6. Add source/dependent-source constraint equations as needed.
  7. Solve the simultaneous equations.
  8. Recover branch currents and powers from the original reference directions.
  9. Verify with an unused KCL equation, KVL, or power balance.
Circuits 1 methods11.3 Worked nodal example

A 12 V source sets the left node to 12 V. A 2 kΩ resistor joins it to unknown node v; a 4 kΩ resistor joins v to ground.

KCL at v, taking currents leaving as positive:

(v − 12)/2000 + v/4000 = 0

Multiply by 4000: 2(v − 12) + v = 0; therefore 3v = 24, so v = 8 V.

Branch from 12 V to v: (12 − 8)/2000 = 2 mA. Branch to ground: 8/4000 = 2 mA. KCL checks.

Circuits 1 methods11.4 Supernode

A supernode surrounds two non-reference nodes joined by a voltage source, because the source current is not directly known from a voltage difference.

  1. Draw a boundary around both nodes and the source.
  2. Write one KCL for currents crossing the boundary.
  3. Add the source constraint, for example v_a − v_b = V_s according to polarity.
  4. Solve the KCL and constraint together.

Do not replace the voltage source with an open circuit in a supernode; it enforces the constraint.

12. Mesh analysisCircuits 1 methods

Circuits 1 methods
Circuits 1 methodsOverview
Circuits 1 methods12.1 Core idea

Mesh analysis assigns circulating currents to the independent meshes of a planar circuit and applies KVL. It is often efficient when current sources are few and series loops dominate.

Circuits 1 methods12.2 Standard recipe
  1. Confirm the circuit is planar; identify the meshes.
  2. Assign mesh currents, usually all clockwise.
  3. Write KVL around each mesh.
  4. For a resistor shared by meshes a and b, the branch current is i_a − i_b in a’s direction; its drop in a’s loop is R(i_a − i_b).
  5. Add source/dependent-source constraints.
  6. Solve simultaneously and recover actual branch currents.
  7. Verify with KCL/KVL or power balance.

Shared-resistor trap: the branch current is the algebraic difference of mesh currents, not automatically either mesh current alone.

Circuits 1 methods12.3 Supermesh

If a current source lies on the boundary between two meshes, neither shared-branch current is known independently in the usual KVL form.

  1. Remove the current source for the purpose of drawing one outer KVL path around the combined meshes.
  2. Write one KVL around that supermesh.
  3. Add the current-source constraint, such as i₁ − i₂ = I_s with signs fixed by the arrows.
  4. Keep dependent sources active and add their controlling equations.

14. Superposition and source transformationCircuits 1 methods

Circuits 1 methods
Circuits 1 methodsOverview
Circuits 1 methods14.1 Superposition

In a linear circuit, any branch voltage or current equals the sum of contributions from each independent source acting alone.

  1. Keep one independent source active.
  2. Set every other independent voltage source to 0 V → replace with a short circuit.
  3. Set every other independent current source to 0 A → replace with an open circuit.
  4. Leave all dependent sources unchanged.
  5. Solve for the desired variable using the same reference direction in every subcircuit.
  6. Repeat for each independent source.
  7. Add the signed contributions.

Do not add powers from subcircuits. Power is quadratic (i²R, v²/R), so superposition applies to v and i, not directly to p.

Circuits 1 methods14.2 Source transformation

A practical voltage source V_s in series with R is externally equivalent to a practical current source I_s = V_s/R in parallel with the same R. Conversely, V_s = I_sR.

  • The two networks have the same terminal v–i behavior.
  • Arrow/polarity must produce current out of the positive terminal in the equivalent model.
  • Internal powers need not match component by component; equivalence is at the terminals.

15. Thévenin, Norton, and maximum powerCircuits 1 methods

Circuits 1 methods
Circuits 1 methodsOverview
Circuits 1 methods15.1 The one-port idea

Remove the load at terminals a–b. Everything remaining is the source network. A linear one-port can be represented as:

  • Thévenin: ideal voltage source V_Th in series with R_Th.
  • Norton: ideal current source I_N in parallel with R_N.

Relationships:

V_Th = v_oc (open-circuit terminal voltage)
I_N = i_sc (short-circuit terminal current, with defined direction)

R_Th = R_N

V_Th = I_N R_N with consistent reference directions

Circuits 1 methods15.2 Finding the equivalent
  1. Identify and remove the load; mark terminal polarity and current direction.
  2. Find V_Th = v_oc with the port open.
  3. Find resistance:
  • Independent sources only: deactivate them (V source → short; I source → open) and look into the port to compute R_eq.
  • Dependent sources present: leave dependent sources active; deactivate independent sources; connect a test source v_test or i_test; calculate R_Th = v_test/i_test using consistent port signs.
  1. If valid and nonzero resistance is known, I_N = V_Th/R_Th; alternatively find short-circuit current directly.
  2. Reconnect the load to the simple equivalent.

A direct R_Th = v_oc/i_sc calculation requires consistent reference directions and a usable nonzero short-circuit current; do not apply it mechanically in degenerate cases.

Circuits 1 methods15.3 Load formulas

For Thévenin source connected to load R_L:

I_L = V_Th/(R_Th + R_L)
V_L = V_Th R_L/(R_Th + R_L)
P_L = I_L²R_L = V_L²/R_L
Circuits 1 methods15.4 Maximum DC power transfer

For a fixed linear resistive Thévenin source with R_Th > 0, load power is maximized when:

R_L = R_Th

Then:

P_L,max = V_Th²/(4R_Th)

At matching, V_L = V_Th/2; half the source-network voltage is across the load. In the simple source-plus-load circuit, efficiency is 50% at maximum load power, not 100%. Maximum power and maximum efficiency are different objectives.

Circuits 1 methods15.5 Worked equivalent example

A 12 V source feeds a divider with R₁ = 4 kΩ on top and R₂ = 2 kΩ to ground. The port is across R₂.

  • Open-circuit voltage: V_Th = 12 × 2/(4 + 2) = 4 V.
  • Deactivate the 12 V source; R₁ and R₂ are parallel at the port: R_Th = 4 kΩ ∥ 2 kΩ = 1.333 kΩ.
  • Norton current: I_N = 4/1333.33 = 3 mA.
  • With R_L = 2 kΩ: V_L = 4 × 2/(1.333 + 2) = 2.4 V.
  • Maximum power occurs at R_L = 1.333 kΩ: P_max = 4²/(4 × 1333.33) = 3.0 mW.

16. CapacitorsTransients

Transients
TransientsOverview
Transients16.1 What capacitance means

An ideal capacitor stores energy in an electric field. Its defining relation is:

q = Cv_C

Read: “charge equals capacitance times capacitor voltage.” Since current is dq/dt, for constant C:

i_C = C dv_C/dt

Read: “capacitor current equals capacitance times the rate of change of capacitor voltage.”

  • Constant capacitor voltage → zero capacitor current.
  • Rapidly changing voltage → larger current.
  • Current direction and voltage polarity must follow passive sign convention for these signs.

Integral form:

v_C(t) = v_C(t₀) + (1/C)∫_{t₀}^{t} i_C(λ)dλ

Read: “present capacitor voltage equals initial voltage plus accumulated capacitor-current area divided by capacitance.” The dummy variable λ avoids using the same symbol for the integration variable and endpoint.

Stored energy:

w_C = ½Cv_C² = q²/(2C) = ½qv_C

Energy is nonnegative for an ideal passive capacitor and does not depend on the sign of voltage.

Transients16.2 Continuity and DC steady state

With finite capacitor current, capacitor voltage cannot jump instantaneously:

v_C(0⁺) = v_C(0⁻)

At long-term DC steady state with constant independent sources, all transient rates have died out, so dv_C/dt = 0 and i_C = 0: model the capacitor as an open circuit.

Capacitor current may jump. Other circuit voltages and currents may also jump unless their own physics prevents it.

Transients16.3 Capacitor waveform example

For C = 100 μF, voltage ramps linearly from 0 V to 5 V in 2 ms, then stays at 5 V.

During ramp: dv/dt = (5 − 0)/(0.002 − 0) = 2500 V/s.
i_C = C dv/dt = 100 × 10⁻⁶ × 2500 = 0.25 A.

After ramp: slope = 0, so i_C = 0; voltage remains 5 V. At 5 V:

w_C = ½(100 × 10⁻⁶)(5²) = 1.25 mJ.

Trap: a nonzero constant capacitor voltage does not imply constant capacitor current; it implies zero current in the ideal model.

17. InductorsTransients

Transients
TransientsOverview
Transients17.1 What inductance means

An ideal inductor stores energy in a magnetic field. Its passive-sign voltage–current law is:

v_L = L di_L/dt

Read: “inductor voltage equals inductance times the rate of change of inductor current.”

  • Constant inductor current → zero inductor voltage.
  • Rapidly changing current → larger voltage magnitude.

Integral form:

i_L(t) = i_L(t₀) + (1/L)∫_{t₀}^{t} v_L(λ)dλ

Stored energy:

w_L = ½Li_L²
Transients17.2 Continuity and DC steady state

With finite inductor voltage, inductor current cannot jump instantaneously:

i_L(0⁺) = i_L(0⁻)

At long-term DC steady state, di_L/dt = 0, so v_L = 0: model the ideal inductor as a short circuit.

Inductor voltage may jump. Do not force resistor currents, source currents, capacitor currents, or inductor voltages to be continuous without a separate reason.

Transients17.3 Inductor waveform example

For L = 10 mH, current ramps from 0 A to 2 A in 1 ms.

di/dt = 2/0.001 = 2000 A/s

v_L = L di/dt = 0.010 × 2000 = 20 V.

If current then remains 2 A, v_L = 0. Stored energy at 2 A:

w_L = ½(0.010)(2²) = 20 mJ.

18. Transient problem setup: before, just after, and finalTransients

Transients
TransientsOverview
Transients18.1 State variables first

The state variables are capacitor voltages and inductor currents. Solve for these first when practical, then recover other variables using KCL, KVL, and element laws.

Three-circuit routine:

  1. t = 0⁻ before switching: use the old switch positions. If the circuit was in DC steady state, replace C with open and L with short. Find v_C(0⁻) and i_L(0⁻).
  2. t = 0⁺ immediately after: use the new switch positions. Preserve v_C(0⁺) = v_C(0⁻) and i_L(0⁺) = i_L(0⁻). A capacitor with known initial voltage can be modeled as a voltage source of that value for this instant; an inductor with known initial current as a current source. Find any required initial derivatives.
  3. t → ∞ after switching: if constant sources and a stable circuit remain, replace C with open and L with short again. Find final values.

At 0⁺, a zero-voltage initially relaxed capacitor behaves like a 0 V source (short at that instant), while a zero-current initially relaxed inductor behaves like a 0 A source (open at that instant). These are instantaneous models, not DC steady-state rules.

Transients18.2 Initial derivatives

Use element laws at 0⁺:

v_C′(0⁺) = i_C(0⁺)/C
i_L′(0⁺) = v_L(0⁺)/L

Find i_C(0⁺) or v_L(0⁺) from the actual post-switch circuit. Never assume the initial derivative is zero merely because the state variable itself is continuous.

19. First-order RC and RL transientsTransients

Transients
TransientsOverview
Transients19.1 Universal first-order template

For a stable first-order circuit with constant post-switch sources, any circuit variable x that approaches a final value follows:

x(t) = x(∞) + [x(0⁺) − x(∞)]e⁻ᵗᐟτ, for t ≥ 0

Read: “x equals its final value plus the initial difference from final, multiplied by a decaying exponential.”

  • x(0⁺) sets the starting point.
  • x(∞) sets the destination.
  • τ sets the speed.
  • The bracket is the amount still to decay at t = 0.
  • The formula also works for decay to zero: set x(∞) = 0.

Time constants:

τ_RC = R_eq C
τ_RL = L/R_eq

R_eq is the Thévenin resistance seen from the storage element’s terminals in the post-switch circuit, with independent sources deactivated and dependent sources preserved.

Transients19.2 First-order recipe
  1. Confirm only one independent energy-storage state remains after reduction.
  2. Use the pre-switch circuit to find the state variable at 0⁻.
  3. Carry it through switching to 0⁺.
  4. Find the post-switch final value.
  5. Remove C or L and find the post-switch R_eq at its terminals.
  6. Calculate τ and substitute into the template.
  7. Recover other variables without assuming they are continuous.
  8. Verify start, end, initial slope, units, and the governing ODE when required.
Transients19.3 Worked RC example

After switching, R_eq = 2 kΩ, C = 100 μF, v_C(0⁺) = 2 V, and v_C(∞) = 10 V.

τ = R_eqC = 2000(100 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.20 s.

Therefore:

v_C(t) = 10 + (2 − 10)e⁻ᵗᐟ⁰·² = 10 − 8e⁻⁵ᵗ V.

Check at t = 0: 10 − 8 = 2 V. Check as t → ∞: exponential → 0, so v_C → 10 V.

At t = τ = 0.20 s: v_C = 10 − 8e⁻¹ ≈ 7.057 V, completing 63.2% of the 8 V rise.

Initial slope from the template:

v_C′(0⁺) = [v_C(∞) − v_C(0⁺)]/τ = (10 − 2)/0.20 = 40 V/s.

This must agree with i_C(0⁺)/C from the circuit.

Transients19.4 RL interpretation

For an RL circuit, use the same template but τ = L/R_eq. Increasing L stores more energy for a given current and slows the response; increasing R_eq shortens τ in this model.

Dimensional check: L/R = (V·s/A)/(V/A) = s.

Transients19.5 Settling and time calculations

The fractional distance still remaining from final is e⁻ᵗᐟτ. To reach a fraction r remaining:

t = −τ ln r

Examples: 10% remaining takes 2.303τ; 1% remaining takes 4.605τ. “Settled” always needs a tolerance; the exponential reaches its mathematical final value only at infinity.

Transients19.6 Pulses and successive switching

At each new switching event:

  1. Evaluate the previous response at the event time.
  2. Use continuity to obtain the new v_C and i_L initial values.
  3. Find the new interval’s final values and equivalent resistance.
  4. Write the template using local elapsed time, for example t − t₁:

x(t) = x_new(∞) + [x(t₁⁺) − x_new(∞)]e^{−(t−t₁)/τ_new}, t ≥ t₁.

Do not reuse the original final value or time constant after the circuit topology changes.

20. Second-order RLC transientsTransients

Transients
TransientsOverview
Transients20.1 Standard forms and vocabulary

Two equivalent standard ODE forms are:

x″ + 2αx′ + ω₀²x = f(t)
x″ + 2ζω_nx′ + ω_n²x = f(t)
  • α (alpha): exponential damping coefficient, units s⁻¹.
  • ω₀ or ω_n: undamped natural angular frequency, units rad/s.
  • ζ (zeta): dimensionless damping ratio.
  • Relationship: α = ζω_n.
  • ω_d: damped oscillation angular frequency in the underdamped case.

The characteristic equation is:

s² + 2αs + ω₀² = 0

Its roots are:

s₁,₂ = −α ± √(α² − ω₀²)

These roots determine the transient shape. The right-hand forcing determines the final/forced response; initial conditions determine constants in the complete response.

Transients20.2 Series and parallel RLC archetypes

For the standard source-free archetypes:

ParameterSeries RLCParallel RLC
ω₀1/√(LC)1/√(LC)
αR/(2L)1/(2RC)
ζ = α/ω₀(R/2)√(C/L)(1/(2R))√(L/C)

For nonstandard networks, derive the ODE from KCL/KVL or reduce to the appropriate equivalent. Do not force a circuit into these formulas merely because it contains R, L, and C.

Transients20.3 Damping classification and response forms
CaseConditionRootsComplete response for constant final x_∞
Overdampedζ > 1 or α > ω₀Two distinct negative real rootsx_∞ + A₁e^{s₁t} + A₂e^{s₂t}
Critically dampedζ = 1 or α = ω₀Repeated s = −αx_∞ + (A₁ + A₂t)e⁻ᵅᵗ
Underdamped0 ≤ ζ < 1 or α < ω₀−α ± jω_dx_∞ + e⁻ᵅᵗ[A₁cos(ω_d t) + A₂sin(ω_d t)]

For underdamped response:

ω_d = √(ω₀² − α²) = ω₀√(1 − ζ²)
T_d = 2π/ω_d

Envelope time constant: τ_env = 1/α = 1/(ζω_n).

ω₀ is not generally the observed oscillation frequency; ω_d is. α controls decay, not oscillation.

Transients20.4 Constants from initial conditions

Use x(0⁺) and x′(0⁺) on the complete response.

Overdamped:

A₁ + A₂ = x(0⁺) − x_∞
s₁A₁ + s₂A₂ = x′(0⁺)

Critically damped:

A₁ = x(0⁺) − x_∞
A₂ = x′(0⁺) + αA₁

Underdamped:

A₁ = x(0⁺) − x_∞
A₂ = [x′(0⁺) + αA₁]/ω_d

Notice the +αA₁ in the derivative equations. It comes from differentiating the exponential factor and is a frequent source of sign errors.

Transients20.5 Worked underdamped ODE example

Suppose a complete source-free response obeys:

x″ + 2x′ + 4x = 0, with x(0⁺) = 4, x′(0⁺) = 0.

Compare with standard form: 2α = 2 so α = 1 s⁻¹; ω₀² = 4 so ω₀ = 2 rad/s. Since α < ω₀, the response is underdamped.

ω_d = √(4 − 1) = √3 rad/s.

Response form: x = e⁻ᵗ[A₁cos(√3t) + A₂sin(√3t)].

At zero: A₁ = 4. Using the initial-derivative formula: A₂ = (0 + 1 × 4)/√3 = 4/√3.

x(t) = e⁻ᵗ[4cos(√3t) + (4/√3)sin(√3t)].

Checks: x(0) = 4; differentiating gives x′(0) = 0; the exponential envelope decays with τ_env = 1 s. In an actual circuit, the two initial conditions must come from the stored state and the 0⁺ circuit—not be assumed.

Transients20.6 Full second-order circuit recipe
  1. Identify the two independent state variables, normally v_C and i_L.
  2. Find their pre-switch values and carry them through continuity.
  3. Draw the 0⁺ circuit and calculate any required initial derivative.
  4. Derive the post-switch ODE using KCL/KVL and element laws.
  5. Normalize the ODE and identify α, ω₀, and ζ.
  6. Find the DC final value if constant sources remain.
  7. Classify damping before choosing a response form.
  8. Apply both initial conditions to the complete response.
  9. Recover other requested variables.
  10. Verify ODE substitution, both initial conditions, final value, units, and limiting behavior.

Exam referenceOverview
  1. Is the circuit purely resistive and static? Start with DC analysis.

  • Rearrange equations and solve two or three simultaneous equations accurately.
  • Read slopes and signed areas from piecewise graphs.
  • Differentiate and integrate exponentials; solve a decay time with ln.
  • Explain current through, voltage across, and power with passive signs.
  • Redraw a circuit by nodes and identify true series/parallel groups.
  • Apply KCL/KVL without changing convention halfway through.
  • Set up nodal, mesh, supernode, and supermesh equations.
  • Keep dependent sources active and write their constraints.
  • Find Thévenin/Norton equivalents and maximum DC load power.
  • Use capacitor/inductor differential and integral laws in both directions.
  • Build 0⁻, 0⁺, and ∞ circuits and obtain initial derivatives.
  • Use the first-order template and recompute τ after switching.
  • Derive a second-order ODE, classify damping, and fit two constants.
  • Verify numerical answers independently rather than only repeating the same calculation.

This guide was built against the Fall 2026 ELEC 275 midterm contract and the local course textbook: Giorgio Rizzoni and James Kearns, Principles and Applications of Electrical Engineering, Seventh Edition (2022), Chapters 1–4. The authoritative scope runs through second-order RLC transients and excludes sinusoidal/phasor and AC-power topics.

The textbook commonly uses ω_n and ζ for second-order systems; many circuit courses also use ω₀ and α. This sheet gives both and connects them by α = ζω_n, ω₀ = ω_n. All circuit laws assume consistent reference directions; capacitor and inductor differential laws use passive sign convention.
